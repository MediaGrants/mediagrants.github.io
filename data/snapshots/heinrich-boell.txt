The Funeral of Ratko Mladić: Between Political Rally and Opportunistic Grotesque

Srđan Milošević

Commentary
Serbia formally bid farewell to Ratko Mladić, who was sentenced in The Hague to life imprisonment for genocide, crimes against humanity and war crimes, without a state funeral. Yet the state organized the transport of his body, the military took part in the ceremony, the head of the Serbian Orthodox Church officiated at the service, and ruling-party structures organized “spontaneous” gatherings along the route. The funeral thus became a political spectacle that reveals not only how far the authorities are willing to push boundaries, but also how much society is willing to accept such conduct.

30 September 2026

The state-organized funeral of Ratko Mladić,

--- relevant lines ---
 commander-in-chief of the Serb forces during the war in Bosnia and Herzegovina, has rightly drawn public attention in Serbia and internationally. Mladić was a military commander who, at the time of his death, was serving a life sentence imposed on him in 2017 by the International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia for war crimes and crimes against humanity, as well as for genocide in Srebrenica. The funeral drew attention because of its sheer scale and its de facto state character, which fell short of a fully official form in only a few details. The direct involvement of the Serbian state was most visible in the army's active participation in the memorial events and the burial. A barely concealed state tribute to the deceased was evident in the presence of a number of government ministers, attending "as private individuals." In the spirit of the "symphonia" between altar and state leadership, the funeral service was conducted personally by the head of the Serbian Orthodox Church. The ruling party, which long ago usurped the state and its mechanisms, organized its phalanxes to "spontaneously" greet, from overpasses and along the road, the vehicle carrying Ratko Mladić's body from Belgrade airport. The body had been flown there from the Netherlands on a Government of Serbia aircraft, accompanied by the Minister of Justice.
As is well known, the senior Nazi military and civilian officials sentenced to death at Nuremberg were for the most part left without a known grave. By contrast, individuals who served fixed prison terms and completed them were, after their deaths, usually buried within the family circle, with modest funeral rites. One exception, which comes somewhat closer to the funeral of Ratko Mladić, is the funeral of Admiral Karl Dönitz, Hitler's successor at the head of the Third Reich. However, the parallel lies primarily in the event as a public (not state) manifestation and in the presence of several thousand citizens and Dönitz's comrades-in-arms, Wehrmacht veterans. Any involvement of the German state was entirely absent in this case. Moreover, the German authorities of the time explicitly ordered that Dönitz be denied all the military honors that an officer of his rank would otherwise have been entitled to. Tunisia

The Orange Grove That Stopped Blooming

Tunisia is one of the most water-scarce countries in the world. Activists are fighting against political silence and calling for greater ecological awareness.

Hannah El-Hitami

When Rawè Kefi was born, her parents named her after a very special phenomenon. In Tunisia, Rawè is the name given to the first rain after a long drought. Rawè Kefi's father runs an orange farm in Menzel Bouzelfa in north-eastern Tunisia. Rawè Kefi remembers how when she was little, he and the other farmers would call each other as soon as the long-awaited rain arrived in December. "May the first rain bless you," they said, giving each other sweets and celebrating together.

The already scarce water resources are increasingly threatened by the climate crisis. Since 2017, there have been more frequent droughts with serious consequences for water and food security. Since President Kais Saied took power in 2019, he has isolated the country leading it into an economic crisis that leaves little room for modernizing agriculture or adapting to climate change. "People need to hear that we are in a state of national emergency," says Rawè Kefi. She wants to raise public awareness of the water crisis among Tunisians. She believes that each and every one should feel responsible – and that every individual can make a difference.
Listening to Rawè Kefi, it's hard not to feel hopeful. She talks fast but in a structured way, smiles a lot and gestures energetically. She has just submitted her doctoral thesis, which deals with art and culture in the context of nature and climate. She recently began teaching environmental humanities at a university in Tunis. This new interdisciplinary research field uses social science perspectives to rethink environmental issues.
She is also a member of the Tunisian Young Climate Change Negotiators. The initiative was founded in 2021 by the Tunisian Ministry of Environment and was the first of its kind in West Asia and North Africa. In order to involve young people in decisions on climate change, participants learn how climate policy works and how it is negotiated at the international level. Once a year, they accompany the Tunisian delegation to international climate summits.
Protests over water shortages have become a defining feature of the country’s summer months – in 2024 alone, 186 protests were recorded .
